# The Narayanan–Kapoor Worldview

*A two-page summary of the position argued across 70 essays at normaltech.ai (formerly AI Snake Oil), Aug 2022 to Aug 2026, by Arvind Narayanan and Sayash Kapoor. Quotes are verbatim from the posts; the full 747-prediction index is the companion document.*

## The thesis in one paragraph

AI is a powerful general-purpose technology, on the order of electricity or the internet. It is not an impending alien intelligence. The core claim is causal, not dismissive: "There is a long causal chain between AI capability increases and societal impact. Benefits and risks are realized when AI is deployed, not when it is developed" (*A guide to understanding AI as normal technology*, Sep 2025). Everything else follows from taking that chain seriously. Capabilities can advance fast while impacts stay slow. Society keeps many points of leverage. And the right posture is resilience and adaptation rather than prediction and prevention.

They insist "normal" does not mean small. "We compare AI to electricity in the second sentence of the essay, and we make clear throughout that we expect it to have profound impacts. Our expectations for AI's impact on labor seem to be at the more aggressive end of the range of expectations from economists." Only in the Silicon Valley bubble, they say, does this count as a skeptical view.

## Five load-bearing beliefs

**1. Diffusion, not capability, is the bottleneck.** Their four-stage model separates methods from applications, and individual adoption from organizational and legal restructuring. Each stage has its own speed limit, and the later stages run at human speed. "With past general-purpose technologies such as electricity, computers, and the internet, the respective feedback loops unfolded over several decades, and we should expect the same to happen with AI as well" (*AI as Normal Technology*, Apr 2025). The corollary is their most-repeated prediction: "we predict that slow diffusion will continue to be the norm in high-consequence tasks." Deployment is not diffusion. Reaching 100M users in two months tells you about buzz, not about workflows changing.

**2. Superintelligence is incoherent, and AGI is not a milestone.** Intelligence is not a scalar. Human ability is not fixed by biology but by mastery of tools, including AI. So there is no threshold that flips the world. "As automation increases, humans will adapt, and will focus on tasks that are not yet automated ... the goalpost of AGI will continually move further away as increasing automation redefines which tasks are economically valuable." A company announcing AGI would not be an actionable event. Recursive self-improvement does not rescue the fast-takeoff story either, because "the external bottlenecks to building and deploying powerful AI systems cannot be overcome by improving their technical design." Clinical trials, regulation, tacit knowledge, and organizational change do not run in a datacenter.

**3. Safety is about control, not alignment, and the risk is people.** Safety is not a model property. Defenses belong downstream, at the attack surfaces, because "whatever harms we are concerned might be possible from a rogue AI will be far more likely at a much earlier stage as a result of a 'rogue human' with AI's assistance" (May 2023). Model alignment is brittle and will always be either too restrictive or too easy to route around. Their striking claim is that "achieving AI control without alignment is not only possible, it doesn't even seem particularly hard, and doesn't require scientific breakthroughs." Poorly controlled AI is commercially unviable, so market forces push toward oversight. "If AI is normal technology, these risks become far more important than the catastrophic ones": inequality, surveillance, power concentration, job disruption, misuse by institutions.

**4. Nonproliferation backfires.** Licensing, compute thresholds, and open-weight restrictions are the policy program of the superintelligence worldview, and they think it is wrong in both directions. It concentrates the market, creates monoculture vulnerabilities, buys at most a few months since "adversaries will match frontier capabilities within months," and requires ever more draconian surveillance as training costs fall. "Nonproliferation ... might help to contain a superintelligence but exacerbate the risks associated with normal technology by increasing market concentration." Resilience, openness, and independent evaluation are the alternative.

**5. The discourse is broken, and measurement of the present beats prediction of the future.** This is the AI Snake Oil inheritance: hype generated by companies and amplified by media, benchmarks that have stopped measuring anything useful, leaderboards gamed by cost-blind agents, a science that may be slowed rather than sped by AI because it amplifies overproduction. "Unless we as a community get much better at measurement of the present and testing competing causal explanations of progress, the level of energy directed at prediction will be misdirected."

## What this means for work

Labor is where the framework is most concrete and least "skeptical." They expect AI to reorganize work on the scale of factory electrification, over a couple of decades, and to be transformative for knowledge work. But no lab milestone causes mass layoffs. Work splits into layers, and the judgment-heavy layers do not thin out: "once a decision can be delegated to AI, it is no longer a source of competitive advantage, and the value of human decision-making migrates upward. Software increases in complexity over time, so there is no ceiling to this process" (Jun 2026). Cheaper output creates derived demand, so more software means more software engineers, even as purely technical skill is devalued relative to engineering judgment. The pattern seen in programming will spread field by field. An increasing share of human jobs will be about controlling AI.

## Falsifiable bets they have put on the record

- AI will not meaningfully outperform trained teams of humans with simple tools at forecasting geopolitical events, or at persuading people to act against their own interests.
- The world in 2027 will look "pretty much like the world in 2025."
- The gap between frontier and publicly available capabilities will stay at months, not years.
- No capability breakthrough will produce rapid job losses; economically transformative AI arrives gradually over the next couple of decades.
- Without regulatory reform, more capable AI will not make legal services cheaper.
- Efforts to simply accelerate scientific production with AI will make the slowdown in science worse.

## Where they say they would be wrong

They are unusually explicit that the framework is a bundle. "If we end up being wrong, it is more likely that we will be wrong wholesale than slightly wrong." The named crux is recursive self-improvement: "it is more likely that we're wrong if RSI is achieved." Note what does not count as evidence against them. A disappointing model release does not vindicate the thesis, and a breakthrough does not refute it, because "the normal technology framework predicts slow timelines ... not because capabilities will hit a wall but because impacts will be slow and gradual even if capabilities continue to advance rapidly."

The lineage is diffusion-of-innovations theory, the economic history of general-purpose technologies, Jeffrey Ding on the innovation-diffusion gap in great-power competition, and a rejection of technological determinism. AI as normal technology is, in their words, "as much a prescription as a prediction": a claim about how the world works, and an argument about how to act so that it stays that way.
