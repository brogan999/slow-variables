# The bottleneck was never the motor.

A general-purpose technology is two things at once: a machine that improves quickly, and a set of buildings, habits, rules and balance sheets that change slowly around it. The headlines follow the first. The economics follow the second. This essay sets out why, what that means for AI, and which numbers the site watches to find out whether it is true.

### Folio I · the name

## Watch the variables that move slowly

Ecologists who study why ecosystems collapse draw a line between two kinds of variable. Brian Walker, Stephen Carpenter and their colleagues, who work on the resilience of rangelands, lakes and fisheries, call the ones people watch fast variables: the size of this year's harvest, say. Underneath sit slow variables, like the amount of organic matter in the soil, which barely move from one year to the next but decide what a dry summer does to the harvest. A fast variable swings with every shock. A slow one decides whether the shock is absorbed or tips the whole system into a different state.

AI has an abundance of fast variables. Benchmark scores, model launches and the length of task a model can finish all move monthly and all make the news. The slow variables are the ones that decide what those headlines amount to: how many firms use the technology, how much work is rebuilt around it, whether output per hour moves, which part of the industry keeps the profit, and how much of the value ends up with the people using it rather than the people selling it. This site is named after them, and it tracks five.

### Folio II · the pace

## Four machines, four clocks

American factories could buy electric power from the early 1880s. It did not show up in manufacturing productivity until the 1920s. Paul David, an economic historian at Stanford, went looking for the reason in 1990, when computers were in every office and still absent from the productivity figures, and found it in the floor plan.

A steam-era factory ran off one engine. A shaft ran the length of the building, belts dropped from it to every machine, and buildings went up several storeys to keep the shafts short. The first electric factories mostly kept this arrangement, with a motor turning the shaft where the engine had been. The large gain came later, from a small motor on each machine. With no shaft to feed, a factory could be a single storey, lighter to build, and laid out around the flow of materials instead of the flow of power. But that was a different building, and David found that the change waited on the old buildings wearing out and on an investment boom in the 1920s to pay for new ones. The motor was ready decades before the factory was.

Everett Rogers came at the same problem from a farm. He grew up in Carroll, Iowa, where his father refused to plant hybrid seed corn for years, though it plainly out-yielded the old seed, until a drought in the 1930s made the difference impossible to miss. Rogers became a rural sociologist, built on a study of how that corn spread through two Iowa towns, and spent his career on why good ideas travel slowly. His answer was that speed depends less on how good an innovation is than on how it looks to the person deciding: whether its advantage is obvious, whether it fits the way they already work, how hard it is to learn, whether it can be tried on a small patch first, and whether they can see it working for someone else.

Put the two together and the shape of the question changes. Asking how fast AI is spreading is like asking how fast electricity spread: the answer depends on which link of the chain you time. So this site splits the chain into its links, the methods, the products built from them, the firms that try the products, and the work that finally gets rearranged, and runs a separate clock on each. The length of task the best AI model can finish half the time, measured by how long it takes a skilled person, has been doubling every [fact:horizon_doubling_days]. The share of American firms using AI stands at [fact:firms_using_ai], and the share of all work hours done with it at [fact:hours_assisted]. Those are not contradictory readings. They are clocks on different machines, and the gap between them is the time it takes to rebuild the factory.

### Folio III · the money

## The money arrives first, and it is supposed to overshoot

Carlota Perez, a Venezuelan economist who studies how technologies get paid for, went through five technological revolutions: the cotton mills that began in Britain in 1771, steam and railways, steel and electricity, oil and the car, and the computer, which she dates to the microprocessor in 1971. Each unfolded in two halves. In the first, installation, investors pour money into the new infrastructure far ahead of anyone's ability to use it, because the payoff to owning a piece of a new kind of network rewards being early more than being right. Canals, railways and fibre-optic cable were all overbuilt, and mostly not by fools. The installation half usually ends in a crash, and then comes deployment: the technology becomes ordinary, the institutions catch up, production rather than finance sets the pace, and the gains spread out of the new industry and into everyone else's.

That makes "are we in a bubble" the wrong question and "which half are we in" the right one, and the second has evidence. The clearest is how far spending on the infrastructure runs ahead of the revenue it is meant to earn. Over the past year the five largest cloud companies spent [fact:capex_to_revenue] as much on buildings and equipment as the AI revenue we can measure, which is the leading labs' sales plus what businesses spend on AI. Not all of that spending is for AI, and not all AI revenue is counted, so the ratio is rough; the direction is not. The second piece of evidence is who is lending. [fact:vendor_financing_4q] of vendor financing was signed over the same four quarters: deals in which the company selling the chips or the computing capacity also lends to, or invests in, the customer buying them. Demand that the seller is helping to fund is real demand, but it is also demand that stops when the funding does.

The site places itself on Perez's curve by a published rule rather than by feel. Spending at least a dollar of capital for each dollar of revenue while vendor financing is still being signed is installation. Spending under half a dollar per dollar while vendor financing shrinks is deployment. Anything else is a turning point, and a change counts only when two quarters in a row agree. By that rule this is installation, which is a statement about where we are, not a forecast of how it ends.

### Folio IV · the spoils

## Inventing it and keeping the money are different problems

David Teece, a business economist at Berkeley, asked in 1986 why the firm that brings an invention to market so often watches customers and imitators do better out of it. His answer had little to do with the invention itself and a great deal to do with two things around it. The first is how easily it can be copied. The second is who owns what the invention needs in order to reach a customer, which he called complementary assets: the manufacturing, the distribution, the service network, the relationships. When copying is easy and those assets are scarce, the profit walks past the inventor and settles with whoever holds them. Being first is an event. Owning the part nobody can route around is a position.

William Nordhaus, a Yale economist later given a Nobel prize for bringing climate change into the economics of long-run growth, asked the next question: of all the value an innovation creates, how much do the innovators keep? For the American economy from 1948 to 2001 his estimate was about two cents in the dollar. The rest went to buyers, as lower prices and better products, because profits from being ahead are competed away as others catch up.

Taken together, they say that a spectacular frontier is entirely compatible with thin and short-lived profits, and that the thing worth tracking is not how clever the models are but which layer of the industry holds its margin, and for how long. The site measures that with gross profit, what is left of revenue after paying to deliver the product, split across the chip makers, the cloud companies and the labs. A year ago the chip makers took [fact:chips_gp_share_year_ago] of the gross profit we can measure. They now take [fact:chips_gp_share_now]. On the other side of the ledger, Americans, added up, say they would need to be paid [fact:consumer_surplus] a year to give up AI chatbots: value users keep rather than value anyone sells. Teece would recognise the first number, and Nordhaus the second.

### Folio VI · the exits

## Write down what would prove you wrong, first

An argument like this one is easy to keep forever, because every slow reading can be called early. The only defence is to name in advance what would change the conclusion and to check it on a schedule. The site's conclusions each come with that list, re-tested every night against the same data, and the list follows this essay.

One of them has already failed. The expectation that profit would drift up from the chip makers toward the model labs, as models became interchangeable, is contradicted by its own series: the chips' share of gross profit rose instead. The monitor says so, with a date, in the changelog, where anyone can check it. That is what a tracker is for.
