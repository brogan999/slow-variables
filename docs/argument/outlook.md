# A reading both sides predicted settles nothing.

Two forecasters can watch the same number climb and both be right, because each of them predicted it. Most arguments about where AI goes next are like that: the evidence each side points to fits the other side's story too. For each question the outlook turns on, this page sets out what named writers argue, what each side expects this site's readings to show, what they show tonight, and the reading that would tell the sides apart.

### Folio I · Can AI do whole jobs, reliably?

## Fast progress on single skills is what both sides expect; whole jobs are the test

The fastest story starts with a loop. Daniel Kokotajlo, who worked on governance at OpenAI, and his fellow forecasters at the AI Futures Project wrote AI 2027, a dated story in which each AI model, a program trained on text and data to write and reason, helps build the next, so progress feeds on itself until AI outdoes people at almost everything by the end of 2027 [cite:ai_2027]. They called 2027 their single most likely year rather than their middle estimate. They pushed their dates later at the end of 2025 and moved them earlier again in 2026, because coding agents, models set to carry out tasks on their own, improved faster than they had expected [cite:ai_futures_update]; their own check finds events running a little slower than the story [cite:ai_futures_q25].

Benjamin Jones, an economist at Northwestern who studies where growth and discoveries come from, answers with arithmetic. Research is a chain of steps and needs every one of them, so making some steps superhumanly fast leaves the rest, still slow, to set the pace, as a line of walkers moves at the speed of its slowest member. In his words, "bottlenecks severely mute the effect of extremely productive AI" [cite:jones_b]. What counts is the share of steps AI can take over.

Stephan Rabanser, Sayash Kapoor, Arvind Narayanan and their colleagues, computer scientists at Princeton who test AI agents, add that getting a task right is not getting it right every time: agents that solve more tasks each year still give different answers when the same task is run again, and a firm hands over work only when the answer is dependable [cite:agent_reliability]. Princeton's tracker of AI agents, which this site reads, scores each agent's accuracy and reliability from nought to one, and across the agents it has tested accuracy has risen by [fact:hal_accuracy_trend] and reliability by [fact:hal_reliability_trend], where a point is a hundredth of that range. AI 2027's story also has agents stumbling in their first years, so the gap settles nothing yet.

[plate:reliability]

The fastest clock settles nothing either. The length of the software tasks AI can finish doubles every [fact:horizon_doubling], at least as fast as AI 2027 assumed, but neither side doubts that single skills race ahead. Whole jobs separate them. On the Remote Labor Index, which scores models on real paid freelance projects completed end to end, the best model manages [fact:rli_record]. The compounding story, as written, needs most of those projects done by the end of 2027; the slowest-step story expects whole projects to trail single skills for years.

### Folio II · Who checks the work?

## Whether checking AI's work becomes a business turns on who must answer for it

Markets run on knowing whom you are dealing with. Gillian Hadfield, who designs legal and regulatory systems for AI, and Andrew Koh, who studies how to design markets, point out that AI agents have none of the identity, registration or records that markets rely on, so each agent must be tied to a person or firm that answers for it, or be given legal standing and assets a court could seize [cite:hadfield_koh]. This site carries their argument one step further: if someone must answer for an agent's work, someone may be paid to check it. In February 2026 Gartner, which advises companies on what to buy, published a guide to the firms that sell platforms for testing and watching AI systems, and since January 2026 insurers have had standard wording to write generative AI, the kind that writes and draws, out of their policies. The rival view is that the labs, the companies that build the most capable models, and the big cloud companies, which rent out computing, give checking away with their own platforms, and no reading yet tells the two apart.

Soren Larson, a co-founder of a company that routes businesses' requests to AI models, who writes about how money moves between the labs and the apps built on them, thinks whoever stands between a lab and its customers gets squeezed. An app reselling a lab's model pays at least the lab's own price per token, the fragment of a word in which the use of a model is sold, and can charge little more than going to the lab directly would cost, so its margin, what is left of revenue after the cost of what was sold, shrinks towards whatever its own additions are worth [cite:larson_squeeze]. His argument rests on one observation: "To my knowledge labs do not offer special wholesale pricing" [cite:larson_squeeze]. Nobody publishes app margins, so it cannot be tested yet.

Platforms face the mirror image. Peyman Shahidi, John Horton and their colleagues, economists who study online markets, argue that agents could slash the cost of searching and bargaining, and expect platforms to set rules for how outside agents may use them, much as websites tell search engines' crawlers, the programs that read the web for them, what they may read [cite:shahidi_etal]. Byrne Hobart and Nikhil Davar, who write The Diff, a newsletter on finance and technology, read Amazon's blocking of Meta's shopping assistant in September 2026 the other way: platforms gain enough from controlling their customers' dealings with AI to keep outside agents out [cite:hobart_inference]. Amazon had already gone to court in November 2025 to keep Perplexity's shopping agent off its site, while Etsy and Walmart agreed to let shoppers buy through OpenAI's chatbot. This site's hand-kept ledger for the past year puts fences at [fact:fences_ledger] and openings at [fact:openings_ledger], but it does not yet sweep both sides under one rule, so it tests nothing yet.

### Folio III · Is adoption slow, or just hard to see?

## Adoption at the pace of past technologies fits both stories, so the argument turns on what the statistics miss

Narayanan and Kapoor's larger argument is that AI is a normal technology, whose big effects on the economy will come over decades, at the pace at which people and institutions change how they work [cite:normal_tech]. The Census Bureau's survey of American businesses fits their account so far: [fact:firms_using_ai] of firms say they used AI in the past two weeks to make what they sell, in line with the pace of past technologies rather than faster. History suggests the harder part comes after arrival. Diego Comin and Martí Mestieri, economists who study how technologies spread between countries, found that new technologies now reach poor countries almost as fast as rich ones, but that poor countries use them far less intensively, and that this gap in depth explains most of the difference in incomes [cite:comin_mestieri].

[plate:adoption]

Diane Coyle and John Lourenze Poquiz, economists at Cambridge who study how the economy is measured, reply that most use of AI is free and its gains come as better work and saved time, which the official statistics do not price, so measured output can lag, or even fall, while people are better off [cite:coyle_poquiz]. Paying consumer subscribers number only [fact:paid_share] of ChatGPT's weekly users. That fits both stories: people may not pay because the tool is not yet worth much, or because its value never passes through a till.

The clearest early sign may be in hiring. Erik Brynjolfsson, Bharat Chandar and Ruyu Chen, economists at Stanford who study AI and work through payroll records, found that workers in their early twenties in the jobs most exposed to AI have fallen behind their less exposed peers, mostly because firms hire fewer of them [cite:canaries]. They describe the pattern without claiming to have proved its cause, so the comparison carries the weight. With each group's employment set at a hundred in November 2022, the young now stand at [fact:junior_gap] against workers over fifty in the most exposed fifth of jobs, and at [fact:junior_gap_control] in the least exposed. If the gap opened everywhere, something other than AI would be the likelier culprit.

### Folio IV · Does work reorganise fast enough to show up in output?

## Two economists have bet against an explosion, and tonight's reading is one both sides expected

The cleanest test here is a bet. Benjamin Moll, an economist at the London School of Economics who studies whole economies, and Alex Imas, who studies behaviour and AI at Chicago Booth, are bullish on what AI will be able to do and still think growth of more than ten percent a year in the next decade or so extremely unlikely, since it would need several unlikely things at once, from fast automation across the economy to AI that automates research itself [cite:moll_imas]. In August 2026 they and five others took the other side of a bet from four people who bet that in some quarter before 2034 American real output per person, output after inflation divided by the population, will stand [test:bet_fast_side] or more above the highest level it had reached a year or more before [cite:bet_fast_side]. Among the four is William MacAskill, a moral philosopher at Forethought, a research group on advanced AI, who with Fin Moorhouse has argued that AI could compress a century of technological progress into a decade [cite:macaskill_moorhouse]. Tonight output per person stands [fact:gdp_pc_over_peak] above that level [cite:moll_imas_bet].

Charles Jones and Christopher Tonetti, economists at Stanford who study long-run growth, supply the slow side's arithmetic: work is a chain of tasks that substitute poorly for one another, so if AI follows the pattern of past automation, growth rises only slowly, to less than three percent a year even by 2075, while in their fastest calibration, in which machines improve everywhere at once, it passes seven percent by 2030 [cite:jones_tonetti]. Nor would a fast path necessarily show early. Anton Korinek, Peter McCrory and colleagues, Charles Jones among them, economists who model growth under advanced AI and several of whom work for or advise Anthropic, turned assumptions about AI into paths for the economy, which they present as scenarios rather than forecasts, and found that "Across scenarios, almost all of the divergence comes after 2027" [cite:korinek_etal].

Even where AI works, the first thing it changes may not be hours. In 1930 John Maynard Keynes, whose work founded modern macroeconomics, suggested that within a century his grandchildren might share out the little work left in a week of about fifteen hours [cite:keynes]. Betsey Stevenson, a labour economist at Michigan, expects the opposite order: the washing machine raised the standard of a clean house long before it shortened the hours spent on housework, and AI, she argues, will first raise how much people are expected to do [cite:stevenson].

The bet settles it by 2034 at the latest. Output per person grew by [fact:gdp_pc_yoy] in the latest year, close to Jones and Tonetti's slow path [cite:jones_tonetti], though a reading like that fits the fastest of Korinek and colleagues' scenarios until about 2027 as well.

### Folio V · Who keeps the money?

## Whether the labs keep their margins turns on how fast open models catch up

Carlota Perez, an economist who studies technological revolutions and the financial bubbles that come with them, found the same sequence in each of the five great surges of technology since 1771: a frenzy of investment that runs ahead of the uses, a crash, then a golden age in which the technology is put to work [cite:perez]. The builders of the infrastructure rarely keep the value, note Arvind Narayanan and Akash Kapur, a senior fellow at New America who studies how AI is governed, with cloud computing and the leading chip foundry, a factory that makes chips for others, as partial exceptions. Selling access to the frontier models, the most capable ones, looks like a commodity business, where buyers choose on price alone, so the labs' likeliest escape, they argue, is to move up the stack, into products and deployments built into their customers' work, where switching is costly [cite:nk_up_the_stack].

Hobart puts the commodity case plainly: once some labs give away open models, whose inner workings anyone may download and run, the price of the cheapest models is set by the chips and power that run them, and a model has more pricing power where it is the best tool for a particular job [cite:hobart_round_trip] [cite:hobart_pacing]. Larson answers with a result from Simon Board and Marek Pycia, economists who study how sellers set prices over time. A seller who could cut its price is usually forced to, by buyers who wait for discounts; but if buyers can walk away to another option instead, "the seller charges a constant price in every period equal to the monopoly price" [cite:board_pycia], the price a seller with no rivals would set. For AI, the open model is the other option: the lab's price holds and the volume leaves, as long as open models improve too slowly for buyers to wait for them [cite:larson_durability].

The readings so far fit both. Open models carry [fact:open_share] of the tokens routed to the busiest models on OpenRouter, a marketplace that passes developers' requests to many models, and trail the best closed ones by [fact:open_lag]. At list prices, among the open models that can be matched with a closed one as capable, the labs' own cheaper models even undercut them, costing a median [fact:open_premium] as much, though that is a single reading so far. Meanwhile the chip makers take [fact:chips_gp_share] of the gross profit, revenue less the direct cost of what was sold, shared by chips, cloud and the labs, and the biggest cloud and platform companies spend [fact:capex_to_revenue] as much a year on buildings and equipment as AI earns in revenue. David Cahn, an investor at Sequoia Capital, has raised his estimate of the revenue AI must earn to pay that spending back every year since 2023, though he now sees in AI's help with writing code a clearer way to earn it [cite:cahn_2026].

[plate:stack]

The price of the best model will settle it. If it falls towards the open models' as the gap closes, the commodity view wins; if it holds while the volume leaves, Larson's does.

### Where they agree

## A handful of points recur across these writers, and not only on one side

Selling access to a model, token by token, is a thin business; someone has to answer for what an AI agent does before it can be trusted with work that matters; checking AI's work becomes a costly task of its own; and people use AI well before firms reorganise around it. Where someone here dissents, the dissent is listed with the point.

### The futures still open

## Crossing progress with rules shows which futures tonight's readings still allow

How fast capability improves and how the rules on deploying AI settle will decide which future arrives. So far the rules have got lighter: the American president ordered moves against state AI laws in December 2025 [cite:eo_14365], Colorado replaced its AI Act with a narrower law on automated decisions in May 2026 [cite:colorado_sb26_189], and the European Union put off its rules for high-risk AI until the end of 2027 and beyond [cite:eu_2026_1744]. Narayanan and Kapoor had argued years earlier that licensing who may build models would not work [cite:nk_licensing].

The grid crosses the two and fills a cell only where a named writer argues it; a cell stays open while none of its signposts is failing its test, and every open cell is marked, never just one. What would close cells, and change the outlook: whole paid projects done end to end, the best model's price holding or falling as open models close in, independent firms winning the checking, platforms fencing or opening, and output per person breaking away before 2030.
