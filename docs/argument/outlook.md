# A reading both sides predicted settles nothing.

Everyone who writes seriously about where AI goes next can point to evidence that fits their story. The trouble is that most of it fits the other story too. This page takes the questions on which the outlook turns: whether AI can do whole jobs reliably, who checks its work, how fast firms take it up, whether work reorganises fast enough to show up in output, and who keeps the money. For each it sets out what named writers argue, what each side expects this site's readings to show and what tonight's readings say, and names the reading that would tell the sides apart.

### Folio I · Can AI do whole jobs, reliably?

## Fast progress on single skills is what both sides expect; whole jobs are the test

The fastest story starts with a loop. Daniel Kokotajlo, who worked on governance at OpenAI, and his fellow forecasters at the AI Futures Project wrote AI 2027, a dated story in which each model helps build the next, so progress feeds on itself until AI outdoes people at almost everything by the end of 2027 [cite:ai_2027]. They called 2027 their single most likely year rather than their middle estimate, and in 2026 they moved their dates earlier again, because coding agents improved faster than they had expected [cite:ai_futures_update].

Benjamin Jones, an economist at Northwestern who studies where growth and discoveries come from, answers with arithmetic. Research is a chain of steps and needs every one of them, so making some steps superhumanly fast leaves the rest, still slow, to set the pace, as a line of walkers moves at the speed of its slowest member [cite:jones_b]. In his words, "bottlenecks severely mute the effect of extremely productive AI". What counts is the share of steps AI can take over.

Arvind Narayanan and Sayash Kapoor, computer scientists at Princeton who study what AI can and cannot do, add that getting a task right is not getting it right every time: agents that solve more tasks each year still give different answers when the same task is run again, and firms hand over work only when the answer is dependable [cite:agent_reliability]. Princeton's tracker of AI agents, which this site reads, shows their accuracy rising by [fact:hal_accuracy_trend] and their reliability by [fact:hal_reliability_trend].

[plate:reliability]

The fastest clock settles nothing. The length of the software tasks AI can finish doubles every [fact:horizon_doubling], at least as fast as AI 2027 assumed, but neither side doubts that single skills race ahead. Whole jobs separate them. On the Remote Labor Index, which scores models on real paid freelance projects completed end to end, the best model manages [fact:rli_record]. The compounding story needs most of them done by the end of 2027; the slowest-step story expects whole projects to trail single skills for years.

### Folio II · Who checks the work?

## Someone has to answer for what an agent does, and that is becoming a market

Markets run on knowing whom you are dealing with. Gillian Hadfield, who designs legal and regulatory systems for AI, and Andrew Koh, who studies how to design markets, point out that AI agents have none of the identity, registration or records that markets rely on, so each agent must be tied to a person or firm that answers for it, or be given legal standing and assets a court could seize [cite:hadfield_koh]. This site carries the argument one step further: if someone must answer for an agent's work, someone will be paid to check it. In February 2026 Gartner, which advises companies on what to buy, published a guide to the firms that sell platforms for testing and watching AI systems, and since January 2026 insurers have had standard wording to write generative AI out of their policies. The rival view is that checking stays a free feature of the labs' and cloud companies' own platforms, and no reading yet tells the two apart.

Whoever stands between a lab and its customers gets squeezed. Soren Larson, a co-founder of an AI gateway company who writes about how money moves between labs and apps, argues that an app reselling a lab's model pays at least the lab's own price and can charge little more than going to the lab directly would cost, so its margin shrinks towards whatever its own additions are worth [cite:larson_squeeze]. It rests on one observation: "To my knowledge labs do not offer special wholesale pricing". Nobody publishes app margins, so it cannot be tested yet.

Platforms face the mirror image. Peyman Shahidi, John Horton and colleagues, economists who study online markets, argue that agents could slash the cost of searching and bargaining, and that platforms which own the customer have every reason to keep other firms' agents out [cite:shahidi_etal]. Amazon went to court in November 2025 to keep Perplexity's shopping agent off its site and in September 2026 blocked Meta's shopping assistant [cite:hobart_inference], while Walmart agreed to let shoppers buy through OpenAI's chatbot. The balance between fences and openings will settle it.

### Folio III · Is adoption slow, or just hard to see?

## Flat measured adoption fits both stories, so the argument turns on what the statistics miss

Narayanan and Kapoor's larger argument is that AI is a normal technology, whose big effects on the economy will come over decades, at the pace at which people and institutions change how they work [cite:normal_tech]. The Census Bureau's survey of American businesses bears them out so far: [fact:firms_using_ai] of firms say they used AI in the past two weeks to make what they sell, the pace of past technologies rather than faster. History suggests the harder part comes after arrival. Diego Comin and Martí Mestieri, economists who study how technologies spread between countries, found that new technologies reach countries ever faster while how deeply they are used lags far behind [cite:comin_mestieri].

[plate:adoption]

Diane Coyle and John Lourenze Poquiz, economists at Cambridge who study how the economy is measured, reply that most use of AI is free and its gains come as better work and saved time, which the official statistics do not price, so measured output can lag, or even fall, while people are better off [cite:coyle_poquiz]. Paying consumer subscribers number only [fact:paid_share] of ChatGPT's weekly users. That fits both stories: people may not pay because the tool is not yet worth much, or because its value never passes through a till.

The clearest early sign may be in hiring. Erik Brynjolfsson, Bharat Chandar and Ruyu Chen, economists at Stanford who study AI and work through payroll records, found that workers in their early twenties in the jobs most exposed to AI have fallen behind their less exposed peers, mostly because firms hire fewer of them [cite:canaries]. They describe the pattern without claiming to have proved its cause, so the comparison carries the weight: the gap between young workers and those over fifty is [fact:junior_gap] in the most exposed fifth of jobs and [fact:junior_gap_control] in the least exposed. If it opened everywhere, something other than AI would be the likelier culprit.

### Folio IV · Does work reorganise fast enough to show up in output?

## Two economists have bet that it will not, and tonight's reading is one both sides expected

The cleanest test here is a bet. Benjamin Moll, a macroeconomist at the London School of Economics, and Alex Imas, who studies behaviour and AI at Chicago Booth, argue that growth far above its usual pace in the next decade would need several unlikely things at once, from fast automation across the economy to AI that automates research itself [cite:moll_imas]. The other side of their bet wins if, in any quarter before 2034, American real output per person stands [test:no_growth_jump] or more above its previous peak [cite:moll_imas_bet]. Tonight it stands [fact:gdp_pc_over_peak] above. Charles Jones and Christopher Tonetti, economists at Stanford who study long-run growth, supply the slow side's arithmetic: work is a chain of tasks that substitute poorly for one another, so if AI follows the pattern of past automation, growth rises only slowly [cite:jones_tonetti].

The fast side does not expect to win early. Anton Korinek, Peter McCrory and colleagues, Jones among them, economists who model growth under advanced AI and several of whom work for or advise Anthropic, turned assumptions about AI into paths for the economy and found that "Across scenarios, almost all of the divergence comes after 2027" [cite:korinek_etal]. Dario Amodei, Anthropic's chief executive, calls his own case for growth that leaps a goal rather than what happens by default [cite:amodei].

Even where AI works, the first thing it changes may not be hours. In 1930 John Maynard Keynes, whose work founded modern macroeconomics, predicted that within a century his grandchildren would share out the little work left in a week of about fifteen hours [cite:keynes]. Betsey Stevenson, a labour economist at Michigan, expects the opposite order: the washing machine raised the standard of a clean house long before it shortened the hours spent on housework, and AI will first raise how much people are expected to do [cite:stevenson].

Output per hour will settle it. It grew by [fact:productivity_yoy] in the latest year, against the [test:productivity_fast] a year this site calls fast, and the fast side needs it to break away before 2030.

### Folio V · Who keeps the money?

## Whether the labs keep their margins turns on how fast open models catch up

Carlota Perez, an economist who studies technological revolutions and the financial bubbles that come with them, found the same sequence in each of the five great surges of technology since 1771: a frenzy of investment that runs ahead of the uses, a crash, then a golden age in which the technology is put to work [cite:perez]. The builders of the infrastructure rarely keep the value, note Arvind Narayanan and Akash Kapur, a senior fellow at New America who studies how AI is governed, with cloud computing and the leading chip foundry as partial exceptions. Selling access to frontier models looks like a commodity business, they argue, so the labs' likeliest escape is to move up the stack into products and deployments built into their customers' work, where switching is costly [cite:nk_up_the_stack].

Byrne Hobart, a former equity analyst who writes The Diff, puts the commodity case plainly: once some labs give their models away, the price of the cheapest models is set by the chips and power that run them, and a model can charge more only where it is the best tool for a particular job [cite:hobart_round_trip] [cite:hobart_pacing]. Larson answers with a result from Simon Board and Marek Pycia, economists who study how sellers set prices over time. A seller who could cut its price is usually forced to, by buyers who wait for discounts; but if buyers can walk away to another option instead, "the seller charges a constant price in every period equal to the monopoly price" [cite:board_pycia]. For AI, the open model is the other option: the lab's price holds, and the volume leaves [cite:larson_durability].

The readings fit both. Open models carry [fact:open_share] of the tokens routed to OpenRouter's busiest models and trail the best closed ones by [fact:open_lag], and at list prices the labs' own cheaper models undercut open models of the same capability, costing a median [fact:open_premium] as much. Meanwhile the chip makers take [fact:chips_gp_share] of the gross profit shared by chips, cloud and the labs, and the biggest cloud and platform companies spend [fact:capex_to_revenue] as much a year on buildings and equipment as AI earns in revenue. David Cahn, an investor at Sequoia Capital, has kept a running estimate since 2023 of the revenue AI must earn to pay back each year's spending, and it has grown every year [cite:cahn].

[plate:stack]

The price of the best model will settle it. If it falls towards the open models' as the gap closes, the commodity view wins; if it holds while the volume leaves, Larson's does.

### Where they agree

## The writers agree more on direction than on speed

Beneath the arguments sit a few points almost everyone here accepts: selling tokens alone is a thin business; the money goes to whoever runs the operating business and answers for it; checking AI's work becomes scarce; and people use AI well before firms reorganise around it. Each has its dissenters, listed with it.
