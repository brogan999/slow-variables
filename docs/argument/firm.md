# A firm rents what it can check and owns what it must answer for.

A firm is a line drawn round some work. Inside it, people are told what to do; outside it, things are bought. Economists have long explained where the line falls by what it costs to deal with strangers. AI models are now sold by the token, a fragment of a word, so a firm can buy thinking by the unit as it buys electricity. This essay asks where the line moves when that is so, and sets out what this site's records show. The title is this site's own reading, argued in the third folio; no one writer cited here makes it whole.

### Folio I · the boundary

## A firm ends where buying gets cheaper than managing

Ronald Coase, a British economist, asked in 1937 why firms exist if prices can settle who makes what. His answer was that using a market is not free. Someone has to find out prices, bargain, and write a contract for every exchange: the transaction costs. Inside a firm a manager's instruction replaces all of that. A firm, "that little planned society" as he later called it, grows until organising one more job inside costs as much as buying it outside [cite:coase].

An AI agent, a model set to carry out tasks on someone's behalf, can search, compare and bargain for almost nothing. Peyman Shahidi, John Horton and their colleagues, who study online markets, called the prospect a Coasean singularity, the point at which dealing costs almost nothing, with a question mark, and expect the line between making and buying to move [cite:shahidi_etal]. One of them works for Amazon and another is a paid adviser to Anthropic. They do not say firms vanish, and they list new frictions agents bring.

Coase's rule has two sides, and cheap intelligence lowers both. Gillian Hadfield, who designs legal and regulatory systems for AI, and Andrew Koh, who studies how to design markets, observe that what stops a firm growing without limit is a set of obstacles that "seem intrinsic to humans but not to AI" [cite:hadfield_koh]. So the rule cannot say by itself whether firms shrink or grow. Something else decides, and the next three folios take the candidates in turn: what a firm knows, what it must answer for, and where its decisions have to be taken.

### Folio II · the leak

## Asking the market for help tells it what you know

Soren Larson, who writes about how money moves between AI labs, the companies that train the largest models, and the apps built on them, turns Coase round. If every firm rents the same models, what separates one firm from another, once trust and customers' attention are set aside, is context: what it knows about its customers, its costs and its plans. To buy from a market a firm has to say what it wants, and saying so reveals context. Even a request for bids tells rivals what the firm thinks matters. On his account the firm exists "to prevent the extraction of its scarce context" [cite:larson_coasean]. The cost of dealing has not fallen. It has changed from the cost of finding a seller into the cost of being seen.

Two older results explain why no market repairs this. Kenneth Arrow, an economist who studied how markets handle knowledge, showed that a buyer cannot tell what information is worth until he has it, and then need not pay [cite:arrow]. George Akerlof, who studied markets in which sellers know more than buyers, showed that when quality cannot be seen, good goods are withdrawn and the market shrinks [cite:akerlof]. A firm cannot sell its context without giving it away, or buy another's without guessing at its quality. So it keeps what it knows and acts on it itself.

The two sides predict different things. Shahidi and his colleagues expect agents to search and bargain across markets for whoever deploys them; Larson expects them to stay inside the firm or be kept off other firms' platforms. This site holds no record that tests it. A count of firms buying knowledge-heavy work through independent agents would settle it, and nobody publishes one.

### Folio III · the liability

## Whoever must answer for the work ends up owning it

Sanford Grossman and Oliver Hart, economists who studied contracts, asked in 1986 what ownership is for. No contract can list everything that might happen. Where it is silent someone has to decide, and they defined owning an asset as holding that right: "Ownership is the purchase of these residual rights" [cite:grossman_hart]. Ownership has a price, since control given to one party is taken from another, so it should rest with the party whose decisions matter most.

Hadfield and Koh point out that instructing an AI is a contract of this kind: what a designer tells a model to aim at can never cover every case [cite:hadfield_koh]. They ask who answers when it goes wrong, and find the law unready, since few legal systems hold anyone liable for acts they could neither foresee nor control.

This site joins the two, and the join is its own reading, not theirs. If an agent's instructions are incomplete, someone must decide in the gaps and bear the loss, and Grossman and Hart's result says that someone should own the operation. Byrne Hobart, who writes The Diff, a newsletter on finance and technology, arrives there from the buyer's side. Once software acts for long stretches with some randomness, the buyer asks whom to sue. A small seller of agents has the least to lose, so the work is bought from a firm with assets, customers and a name to lose, and a seller sure of its software may do best to own the business that uses it [cite:hobart_sued]. He notes in passing that insurance written for agents is beginning to be sold. Christian Catalini, Xiang Hui and Jane Wu, economists who study the cost of verifying work, add that doing gets cheaper with computing power while checking is held up by people's time, so the profit moves to whoever can verify the work and stand behind it [cite:catalini].

The rival view is that insurance and contract will carry the liability, so nobody need own anything. Over the past year this site has recorded [fact:insurer_ledger] moves by insurers to write AI's mistakes out of their standard policies. One of those moves was an insurer selling separate cover for AI as it took it out of the standard policy. Both sides expect exclusions first. They part on what comes next: cover for AI sold widely, which would favour the rival, or operators absorbing the risk. Nobody publishes the premiums written for such cover, so tonight's reading cannot tell them apart.

### Folio IV · the edge

## Knowledge can be sent to the centre; acting on it sometimes cannot

Friedrich Hayek, an economist who studied how prices carry what people know, argued in 1945 that much of the knowledge an economy runs on is "the knowledge of the particular circumstances of time and place" [cite:hayek], which no central office can collect, so decisions belong with the person on the spot.

Erik Brynjolfsson, who directs Stanford's Digital Economy Lab, and Zoë Hitzig, who studies AI's effects on society, argue that AI undoes much of this. Hitzig worked at OpenAI when they wrote and has since joined a research institute run by Anthropic. It records what used to go unwritten and lets a head office process far more, so they predict "larger average firm size, greater industry concentration, and reduced local managerial autonomy" [cite:brynjolfsson_hitzig]. This site sets beside them a finding they do not cite. Rich Sutton, a computer scientist who studies how machines learn, found that over decades of AI research, methods built on what experts know were beaten by general methods that improve as computing gets cheaper [cite:sutton]. He was writing about research, not firms; the reading across is this site's.

Chris Walker, who writes on the economics of unwritten knowledge, replies that writing knowledge down for a model is neither free nor complete. Choosing what a model needs to know for each task takes local judgement and has to be done again as the work changes. "The knowledge problem has not been solved", he writes; it has moved upstream [cite:walker_context]. Larson grants that context can be gathered and still ends at the edge, the place where the work is done: running a model costs energy and time, and a car at speed cannot wait for an answer from a distant data centre [cite:larson_hayek].

The camps agree on more than their conclusions suggest. Both draw a line between what can be recorded and what cannot, and both leave time-critical decisions at the edge: Brynjolfsson and Hitzig themselves list decisions that must be taken faster than a message can travel, beside rare cases, bodily skill and whatever the law reserves for people. They differ on whether the line moves. Brynjolfsson and Hitzig think the exceptions may shrink as models improve; Walker expects each new ability to open new work that needs local judgement. Delay is the floor neither side disputes, and energy is Larson's addition. Brynjolfsson and Hitzig name the test, whether firms that adopt AI move decisions to the centre; Walker's own dated predictions turn on firm size, and no one holds a series that reads either.

### Folio V · the rent

## Most firms should rent intelligence; a few should own it

There are two reasons to rent. The lead is short: open models, whose trained settings are published for anyone to run, trail the best closed ones by [fact:open_lag], so a firm that trains its own is buying something that loses value fast. And most firms see nothing in their own work that others do not see: in a simulation of his own, Larson found that a system trained on other firms' results did better than one trained on a firm's own history [cite:larson_router]. Luis Garicano, an economist who studies how firms organise what they know, and Jesús Saa-Requejo put it plainly: "Intelligence is not what stops most organisations from doing what they want to do" [cite:garicano_theses].

The risk in renting is set out by Garicano and Saa-Requejo too. A supplier of rented intelligence can raise its price once a firm's work depends on it, and can learn the firm's business by watching how it is used [cite:garicano_holdup]. That is Larson's leak again, with the lab as the party watching. Their remedy is not to own the model. It is to rent it, to own the rules and records that sit above it, and to keep a second supplier within reach. So the answer turns on what is scarce in the firm's own work.

[plate:regimes]

Clayton Christensen, who studied why leading firms lose their lead, gave the general rule: when one part of a product becomes good enough and interchangeable, the profit moves to the neighbouring part that still limits performance [cite:christensen]. Intelligence by the token is becoming that part. Larson cautions against his own case: owning every step does not make a firm safe, because AI also makes it cheap to stitch separate pieces into something good enough [cite:larson_vertical].

### Folio VI · the residue

## Some things do not get cheaper when intelligence does

Philippe Aghion, Benjamin Jones and Charles Jones, economists who study growth, argued that when tasks depend on one another, growth may be held back by "what is essential and yet hard to improve" [cite:aghion_jones_jones]. Value collects at whatever stays slow, and inside a firm several things do.

Attention: a customer has the same hours in the day whatever a model costs. Ben Thompson, who writes on technology strategy at Stratechery, showed how firms that own the relationship with users make their suppliers interchangeable [cite:thompson]. Accountability: a model cannot be sued, fined or struck off, and a firm can. Trust: one of the answers Akerlof named for quality nobody can see was a name with something to lose. Permission: licences, regulators and courts decide who may act, and Brynjolfsson and Hitzig count them among the limits on their own prediction. And unwritten judgement: knowing what good work looks like in a trade.

The last is the one most likely to give way. If models come to learn on the job, what is unwritten today is recorded tomorrow, and Walker says he is wrong if general agents manage complex work inside firms without continuing human help by 2028.

### Folio VII · the limit

## In the limit, a firm is a name that can be sued, holding what cannot be bought

What follows is this site's judgement. It joins arguments that each have a test into a picture that has none.

Carry each argument to its end and the firm stops being a body of people who do the work. It specifies: it decides what is worth doing and what counts as done. It holds scarce things: customers' attention and trust, licences, sensors, sites with power. It bears risk: it is the legal person that pays when the work is wrong. And it rents its thinking by the token from whoever sells it cheapest, apart from small models it runs on the spot where delay or secrecy demands.

On the shape of such firms the theories part, and this site keeps all three. Brynjolfsson and Hitzig expect larger firms and more concentrated industries, and Hadfield and Koh see a pull the same way. Dwarkesh Patel, a podcaster who interviews AI researchers, writing with two co-authors and putting his own confidence low, expects much larger firms and thinks a single one is not inevitable, because "The market continues to serve as the grounding outer loop" [cite:patel]. Walker and Larson each expect a barbell. Walker's has very large firms where the work is generic and many small ones close to each trade; Larson's has very large firms that keep what they know to themselves and many small ones. Larson also expects groups that own many businesses and share what each learns to be worth more than their parts [cite:larson_conglomerate]. Firm size by industry, and the value of such groups against their parts, would separate these, and this site cannot yet read either.

Who profits, on this reasoning, is not mainly the seller of tokens. In Larson's own model a lab captures about a quarter of what its tokens are worth to the buyers, and less as open models catch up [cite:larson_complexity]. The rest goes to the owners of what is scarce beside the intelligence, and, where firms compete, to their customers.

### Folio VIII · the record

## Buyers are acting on this before anyone can show it pays

Several of this site's records bear on the argument. Roll-ups, buyers that gather many small firms into one and plan to run them with AI, bought [fact:rollup_service_deals] firms that sell services, such as accounting practices and letting agents, in the past year that this site could find, against [fact:rollup_service_deals_year_ago] the year before. The count comes from one search of the larger roll-ups this site follows, so it is a floor; older announcements are the likeliest to have been missed, which flatters the rise; and some buyers announce firms as joining them without stating a stake. What they buy, on this site's reading, is customers and licences. Their purchases of software products, [fact:rollup_software_deals] in the same year, are counted apart.

The labs are moving the same way. They and the companies that sell computing made [fact:lab_deals_now] purchases in the layers above them, hirings of whole teams included, in the latest year this site has searched. Among them is a firm that fits AI into other companies' work, which OpenAI agreed to buy as it launched a company that it says it majority-owns and controls. Anthropic formed a services venture with outside investors in the same month; the reports this site holds do not say who owns how much, so it is not in this reading. That count's rule leaves out ordinary businesses, so it would miss a lab buying an accounting practice.

Suppliers are binding customers without buying them. Commitments signed in which a seller of chips or computing also funds the lab that buys from it, by a stake, a guarantee, a backstop or a contract to pay whether or not the capacity is used, came to [fact:seller_funded] over the past four quarters. That is the face value promised, not money paid, and a few very large deals make up most of it. On this site's reading it is a way to bind a customer short of owning it; only the stakes carry any right to decide.

The check matters more than the task. This site's census screens American knowledge work for whether it could be handed to software. On its count, the payroll that passes the screen is far smaller than the payroll held back for one reason only: no existing check settles whether the work was done right. The figures are on the census page, since a screen scored by models is not a reading this essay can test.

None of this shows that owning pays. [fact:firms_using_ai] of American firms say they used AI in the past fortnight, a pace this site calls normal. The site holds no margin for any roll-up after a purchase. The census could not measure whether an owner keeps a saving, and it was scored by three AI models, one of them made by Anthropic, whose model also drafted this page. The site's figures on firm size run years late, and nothing here records where decisions are taken. The site's own leans, a model's judgement on claims too early to test, run against two threads of this essay: they doubt that AI's work will increasingly be bought through firms that can be sued, and that groups of pooled businesses will be valued above their parts. A count of purchases records what buyers believe. Each claim below says what would prove it wrong.
