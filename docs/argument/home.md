# The bottleneck was never the motor.

American factories could buy electric power from the early 1880s, and it did not show up in the productivity statistics until the 1920s. The motors worked. What took four decades was rebuilding the factories around them, and nobody tears down a factory that is still standing. This site tracks AI on the same bet: the technology is fast, and the things it has to change are slow.

### Folio I · the pace

## Four machines, four clocks

A steam-era factory ran off one engine. A shaft ran the length of the building, belts dropped from it to every machine, and the floor plan was drawn around the shaft. Early electrification mostly swapped the engine for a big motor and left the shaft where it was. The real gain came from a small motor on every machine, which let a factory be one storey, laid out around the flow of work instead of the flow of power. That needed a new building, and firms replace buildings when the old ones wear out.

Paul David, an economic historian at Stanford, told this story in 1990 to explain why computers were not showing up in productivity either. The lag, he argued, was not a sign the technology was weak; it was the time the old plant took to wear out and be replaced. Everett Rogers, a sociologist who grew up on an Iowa farm where his father held out for years against a visibly better seed corn, found the general rule: things spread as fast as the people who must adopt them can see the advantage, try it on a small scale, and fit it into the way they already work.

So this site splits the chain into links (the methods, the products, the firms that try them, the work that gets rearranged) and times each on its own clock. METR, an independent research group that tests AI models, measures the length of software task the best model can finish half the time, counted in how long it takes a skilled person. That length has been doubling every [fact:horizon_doubling_days]. The share of firms using AI and of work hours done with it move on a different clock entirely. They are not in conflict. They are measuring different machines.

### Folio II · the money

## The money arrives first, and it is supposed to overshoot

Carlota Perez, a Venezuelan economist who studies how technologies get financed, went through five technological revolutions, from the first cotton mills in 1771 to the microprocessor in 1971, and found the same two halves in each. First comes installation: money chasing returns pours into the new technology and pays for infrastructure long before anyone can use it all. Then a turning point, usually a crash, and then deployment, when the technology turns ordinary and its gains spread into everyone else's accounts.

Which half we are in is a question with evidence. Over the past year Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, Meta and Oracle spent [fact:capex_to_revenue] as much on buildings and equipment as the AI revenue we can measure: the leading labs' sales plus one survey of what American businesses spend on generative AI. And [fact:vendor_financing_4q] of commitments were signed over the same four quarters in deals where the seller also funds the buyer, such as a cloud contract with a company that owns a stake in the lab, or a chip maker lending to its customer.

None of that says bubble. It says installation, which is a statement about where we are, not about how it ends.

### Folio III · the spoils

## Inventing it and keeping the money are different problems

David Teece, a business economist at Berkeley, asked in 1986 why the firm that brings an invention to market so often watches its customers and imitators do better out of it. His answer had little to do with the invention. When it is easy to copy, the money goes to whoever owns what it needs to reach a customer: the manufacturing, the distribution, the relationships. Being first is an event. Owning the part nobody can route around is a position.

William Nordhaus, a Yale economist later given the Nobel memorial prize in economics, measured how much of the value innovation creates stays with the innovators as profit. For the American economy from 1948 to 2001 his answer was about two cents in the dollar; the rest went to buyers, as lower prices and better products. If AI follows that pattern, a dazzling frontier is compatible with thin, short-lived profits, and the thing to watch is who holds their margin. Measured by gross profit, what is left after paying to deliver the product, the two big chip makers, NVIDIA and AMD, took [fact:chips_gp_share_year_ago] of the gross profit we can measure a year ago, against Microsoft's cloud business and the leading labs. They take [fact:chips_gp_share_now] now.

### Folio IV · the exits

## Write down what would prove you wrong, first

Every reading here is graded by a rule the site publishes and can change only in public, with a written reason, so a number cannot quietly be graded kindly after the fact. The conclusions work the same way: each comes with the evidence that would overturn it, re-checked every night. One has already failed. The idea that profit would drift up from the chip makers toward the model labs as models became interchangeable is contradicted by its own series: the labs' share grew, but the chip makers' share grew too. The Who profits page says so in its headline. That is what a tracker is for.
