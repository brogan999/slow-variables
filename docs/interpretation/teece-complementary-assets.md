# Who profits from an innovation

A note on how the tracker's capture lens reads through David Teece's "Profiting from technological innovation" (*Research Policy*, 1986) and the later work on dynamic capabilities. It organises what the tracker measures; it supplies no figures.

**The innovator is not the default winner.** Teece's question is why the firm that invents something often fails to keep the returns from it. His answer turns on two things outside the invention: how well the innovation can be protected, and who owns the assets it needs to reach a customer. Neither is a property of the technology, which is why a lens that only tracks capability tells you nothing about who gets paid.

**Appropriability.** Where imitation is cheap and protection weak, the surplus flows past the innovator to whoever holds the scarce input or the customer. The tracker reads this through the open-versus-closed gap, the open-weights lag and the price of capability: a fast-falling price at a fixed capability is a weak appropriability regime made visible.

**Complementary assets.** An innovation reaches a customer through manufacturing, distribution, support and trust, and each of those can be specialised to it or generic. When a complementary asset is both specialised and tightly held, its owner takes the rent. The layer structure is the tracker's list of candidate complementary assets, and the margin stack is the reading: the layer whose share of stack gross profit rises is, on Teece's account, the one holding the asset the others cannot substitute.

**Dominant design.** Before a dominant design settles, competition is about the shape of the product and returns are thin; after, competition moves to cost and scale, and the assets that matter change. The tracker cannot date a dominant design, so it watches the proxies that would move around one: concentration inside a layer, token share across models, and whether buyers use one vendor or several.

**What the tracker cannot see.** Teece's argument is about bargaining between firms, and bargains are struck in contracts that are not public. The tracker sees their consequences in filed segment results and disclosed deals, which is why the capture lens reports direction rather than a share of surplus, and why a layer with no filed figures is drawn as unmeasured rather than estimated.
