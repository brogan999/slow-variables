# Who kept the money in past technology waves

A note on the historical record behind Folio IV of the argument: at each stage of earlier technology waves (invention, bringing it to market, building the shared infrastructure, applying it), who kept the profit, and where the usual stories go further than their sources. Written from the works listed at the end, each fetched on 24 Sep 2026. It organises what the tracker reads; the site's own figures come from its observations, not from here.

**The base rate.** William Nordhaus estimated that innovators captured about two cents of every dollar of social value their innovations created in the American economy from 1948 to 2001, with a range of roughly one to three cents across his estimates. David Teece explained where the rest goes: when an invention is easy to copy and the assets needed to sell it (manufacturing, distribution, service, relationships) belong to someone else, the profit settles with the owners of those assets. Teece's full paper is not freely available; its abstract is, and it states the complementary-assets argument. The often-repeated examples of EMI's scanner, IBM's personal computer and Xerox's laboratory are best treated as later retellings unless read in the paper itself.

**Invention.** James Watt and Matthew Boulton were paid through a patent that Parliament extended to 1800 and royalties set at a third of the value of the fuel an engine saved over the older Newcomen design. F. M. Scherer's reckoning, quoted by Boldrin and Levine, is that most of their profit came from royalties, not from making engines; outside contractors made most of the parts. The case is one where the inventors did keep a great deal, through a legal monopoly rather than the business itself. Michele Boldrin and David Levine, who tell the story in detail, argue that the patent held back the engine's improvement.

**Bringing it to market.** Eight staff left William Shockley's laboratory in 1957 to found Fairchild Semiconductor, backed by Fairchild Camera and Instrument; Robert Noyce and Gordon Moore left Fairchild to found Intel in 1968. Samuel Insull, who came to America as Edison's private secretary, built a Chicago utility and a web of holding companies that went into receivership in 1932; three juries acquitted him, so accounts that call him a fraudster go beyond the record.

**Building the infrastructure.** Andrew Odlyzko's papers on the British Railway Mania find that investors in railway shares of the 1840s lost heavily, roughly a third of the money put into British railways by 1850 by his estimate in *Collective Hallucinations*, while the mania tripled the country's railway mileage and, he judges, quite likely left the nation better off. The earlier railway boom of the 1830s made money for its investors, so "infrastructure investors always lose" is too strong. He finds fraud was not the main cause; overbuilding on inflated expectations was. On the late-1990s fibre build-out, he finds traffic roughly doubled each year, not the widely believed doubling every hundred days, leaving large overcapacity; WorldCom's collapse was also an accounting fraud, as he notes elsewhere. The widely repeated share of fibre left unlit does not appear in these sources.

**Applying it.** Ford's moving assembly line dates from 1913; the Henry Ford museum notes that mass production needed electricity and that the Highland Park plant generated its own power. Standard Oil used railway rebates to take over most of its Cleveland rivals and came to refine most of America's oil. Emek Basker traces Walmart's advantage to logistics, distribution and inventory control built on computing, and to the economies of scale, scope and density they allowed. Marc Levinson's history of the shipping container shows how a new piece of infrastructure paid off only once ports, shippers and unions reorganised around it; it does not show that Malcom McLean kept a fortune.

**The exceptions.** The seller of equipment to a building boom can do very well while the boom lasts: Cisco was briefly the world's most valuable company in March 2000. Chokepoints can hold for decades: the Bell System until its break-up in 1982 to 1984; Microsoft, which a federal court found held more than nine in ten Intel-compatible PC operating systems; Google's search-ad auction, a second-price refinement Google introduced in 2002 of the pay-per-click auction Overture pioneered in 1997; Overture later switched to Google's version.

**Nations and finance.** Jeffrey Ding argues that where general-purpose technologies (steam, electricity, computing: technologies that spread into every industry) are adopted widely matters more for which great power rises to economic leadership than where they are first introduced. William Janeway argues that the state and speculative finance, bubbles included, have together paid for the build-out of new technologies.

**Three more frames.** Clayton Christensen's *The Innovator's Dilemma* explains why well-run incumbents can lose to entrants serving a market they ignored. Alfred Chandler's *The Visible Hand* shows managerial coordination replacing the market in American business, from the railways of the 1840s on. Robert Gordon's "Is U.S. Economic Growth Over?" argues that many of the great inventions of the second industrial revolution could happen only once, and that the computer revolution produced only a short revival of growth.

**What would break the pattern this time.** If model makers become a chokepoint that users cannot route around, the innovators' share could be far larger than the historical base rate; if open models keep closing on closed ones, the base rate should hold. The site tracks both through its open-weights lag and its gross-profit stack, and through the value folio of its outlook.
