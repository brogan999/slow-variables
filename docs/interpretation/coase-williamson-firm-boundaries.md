# What a firm makes and what it buys

A note on how the tracker reads the boundaries of the firm through Ronald Coase and Oliver Williamson. It organises what the tracker measures; it supplies no figures.

**Firms exist because markets cost something to use.** Ronald Coase, a British economist who studied how businesses are organised, asked in "The Nature of the Firm" (1937) why any activity is run by command inside a company when prices could coordinate it outside. His answer was that using the market has costs: finding who sells what, agreeing a price, writing and enforcing a contract. A firm grows until organising one more activity inside it costs as much as buying it outside. Anything that lowers the cost of using the market shrinks firms; anything that lowers the cost of managing inside them grows them. He won the Nobel prize in economics in 1991.

**What makes buying risky.** Oliver Williamson, an American economist who studied contracts and organisations, turned Coase's idea into a test in Markets and Hierarchies (1975) and The Economic Institutions of Capitalism (1985). People cannot foresee everything, and some will exploit a gap in a contract when they can. That matters most when an investment is specific: worth much less in any other use, like a factory built next to a single customer or a system trained on one firm's work. A buyer who depends on a specific asset owned by someone else can be held up, squeezed once it is too late to switch. So activities built on specific assets, repeated often and hard to describe in advance, move inside the firm; generic ones, easy to specify and bought from many sellers, stay in the market. He shared the Nobel prize in economics in 2009.

**How AI pulls both ways.** AI lowers some of the costs Coase named: searching, comparing, drafting and checking contracts, watching whether work was done. On his account that pushes activity out to the market and favours smaller firms buying more of what they use. But AI also creates specific assets: a model tuned to one firm's work, the record of how its people decide, the integrations that tie a tool into its systems. On Williamson's account those pull activity back inside, or into long contracts that protect whoever made the investment.

**What to own and what to buy.** Read together, the two give a rule a company can apply to itself. Own what is specific to it and sits on the input that binds, because a supplier who controls that can take the profit. Buy what is generic and getting cheaper, because owning it adds cost without adding protection. The rule moves with the bottleneck: an input that is scarce and specific today can become generic tomorrow, and what was worth owning becomes worth buying.

**What the tracker reads.** The acquisitions ledger records labs and compute companies buying the firms that sell them data, tools and services, which on Williamson's account is integration to escape hold-up. The labs' move from selling access by the token into deployments inside their customers is the same test run from the other side. The census of which tasks can be handed over reads where the market can now reach inside the firm.

**What the tracker cannot see.** Contracts between firms are mostly private, so the tracker sees integration only when it becomes a disclosed deal or a filed segment. It cannot measure how specific an asset is, only the moves that suggest someone thinks it is.
