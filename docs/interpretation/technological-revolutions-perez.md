# Framework Extraction: *Technological Revolutions and Financial Capital* — Carlota Perez (2002)

**Section:** 3 — Modern Era (2000s–2020s)
**Project:** Venture Capital Frameworks Research Project

---

## 1. THE MODEL

**Core falsifiable claim:** Every technological revolution follows a recurring ~50–60 year "great surge" that unfolds through a fixed sequence of four phases — irruption, frenzy, synergy, maturity — divided at the midpoint by a "turning point" (financial crash + institutional reckoning) that separates an *installation period* (where financial capital leads, speculation dominates, and infrastructure is built) from a *deployment period* (where production capital leads, regulation recouples finance to the real economy, and a "golden age" of broadly shared prosperity becomes possible). The pattern has repeated five times since the 1770s and is structurally inherent to capitalism, not contingent on specific technologies or political regimes.

**Mechanism:** Perez identifies two fundamentally different kinds of capital whose shifting relationship drives the cycle:

**Financial capital** seeks maximum short-term returns, is inherently mobile, and is indifferent to the productive purpose of its deployment. It functions as the agent of Keynesian speculation — buying assets for resale rather than for the income they generate. During the installation period, financial capital is the protagonist: it discovers the technological revolution, falls in love with its potential, and pours money into new ventures and infrastructure with escalating speculative intensity. Financial capital's indifference to productive purpose is precisely what allows it to fund transformational investments whose economic value cannot yet be calculated.

**Production capital** is embedded in specific industries, committed to long-term value creation, and oriented toward the actual deployment of technology into goods and services. It is patient, geographically rooted, and concerned with organizational capability. During the deployment period, production capital is the protagonist: it takes the infrastructure built during the frenzy and converts it into a functioning new economy with new business models, management practices, and consumption patterns.

The four phases and the turning point between them:

**Phase 1 — Irruption.** A "big bang" announces the new technological revolution (Arkwright's mill 1771, Rocket steam engine 1829, Carnegie Bessemer plant 1875, Ford Model T 1908, Intel microprocessor 1971). New industries emerge alongside the exhausted mature industries of the previous revolution. Financial capital begins shifting from the old paradigm to the new. Early venture capital and risk-tolerant investors fund the pioneers. Two paradigms coexist tensely — the old still dominant, the new still marginal.

**Phase 2 — Frenzy.** Financial capital decouples from production capital and "governs the casino." Speculation becomes self-reinforcing as rising asset prices attract more capital. The new techno-economic paradigm's infrastructure gets massively overbuilt (canals, railways, fiber optic cables) because speculators profit from financial asset appreciation regardless of whether the underlying real assets generate returns. Income inequality widens as financial gains concentrate. Regulatory frameworks designed for the old paradigm are overwhelmed or deliberately dismantled. The frenzy installs the infrastructure the deployment period will need — but at the cost of a bubble that must eventually burst.

**The Turning Point.** The bubble collapses. A recession follows, sometimes severe (1929–33, 2000–02). Institutional reckoning ensues: fraud is exposed, regulations tightened, leadership changes. The key dynamic is *recoupling*: financial capital, chastened by losses, is brought back into alignment with production capital through a combination of regulatory reform, political negotiation, and the sheer attractiveness of profitable investment in the now-mature infrastructure. This is not automatic — it requires active institutional innovation. If the turning point fails (as in the 1870s–90s "Gilded Age"), the result is decades of instability rather than a golden age.

**Phase 3 — Synergy.** The new techno-economic paradigm diffuses across the entire economy. The infrastructure built during the frenzy is now exploited for productive purposes. New business models, organizational forms, and consumption patterns spread. Growth is broad-based, employment rises, inequality narrows. Financial capital supports productive investment because the returns from deploying proven technology into expanding markets are attractive and relatively predictable. This is the "golden age" — the Roaring Twenties deployment of electrification, the 1950s–60s deployment of mass production, the period many hoped would follow the dotcom crash.

**Phase 4 — Maturity.** The revolution's potential is substantially exhausted. Markets saturate, productivity gains diminish, returns decline. Financial capital, finding diminishing opportunities in the aging paradigm, begins searching for new outlets — initially in financial innovation and geographic expansion, then in the embryonic technologies that will constitute the next revolution. The seeds of the next great surge are planted in the soil of the current one's decline.

The entire sequence constitutes what Perez calls a **techno-economic paradigm** — not just a set of technologies but a comprehensive "common sense" for how to organize production, manage firms, structure markets, and regulate economies. Each revolution brings a new low-cost input (cotton, coal, steel, oil, microchips), new infrastructure (canals, railways, electrical grids, highways, the internet), and new organizational principles (the factory, the railway company, the corporation, the assembly line, the network). The paradigm doesn't just change what is produced; it changes *how everything is produced.*

## 2. THE EVIDENCE

**Finding 1 — Five revolutions, same pattern.** Perez maps the identical four-phase sequence across five technological revolutions spanning 230 years. Each begins with a "big bang" innovation, proceeds through speculative frenzy and infrastructure overbuilding, passes through a financial crash and institutional turning point, and culminates in a deployment-phase golden age. The Industrial Revolution (1771), Age of Steam and Railways (1829), Age of Steel and Electricity (1875), Age of Oil and Mass Production (1908), and Age of Information and Telecommunications (1971) all conform to the pattern. The regularity is the central empirical claim: this is not a theory about any single revolution but a meta-theory about the structural coupling of technological and financial dynamics in capitalism.

**Finding 2 — Financial capital and production capital decouple during installation, recouple during deployment.** The most distinctive empirical signature is the behavior of financial capital across the cycle. During irruption, financial capital discovers the new paradigm and begins funding it. During frenzy, it decouples entirely from productive purpose — asset prices diverge from cash flows, financial innovation proliferates, and "paper wealth" creation vastly exceeds real wealth creation. After the crash, the turning point forces recoupling: Perez documents how the Railway Mania crash of the 1840s, the 1929 crash, and the 2000 dotcom crash each preceded periods of regulatory reform (Railway Acts, Glass-Steagall/SEC, Sarbanes-Oxley) that realigned financial incentives with productive investment. The canal mania of the 1790s built Britain's canal network; the railway manias built railway systems on both sides of the Atlantic; the 1920s electrification bubble built power grids; the dotcom bubble built broadband infrastructure.

**Finding 3 — Each revolution generates a new "techno-economic paradigm" that reshapes all sectors.** Perez demonstrates that the impact of a technological revolution extends far beyond its core industries. Mass production didn't just create cars — it generated suburban development, highway systems, supermarkets, consumer credit, and the managerial corporation. The information revolution didn't just create computers — it generated just-in-time manufacturing, global supply chains, network organizations, and the platform economy. The new paradigm becomes "common sense" — the unquestioned framework within which all economic actors operate. Firms that resist the new paradigm are eliminated by competition; those that adopt it thrive. This is a techno-economic analogue to Kuhn's scientific paradigm shifts.

**Finding 4 — The turning point is where policy matters most.** Whether the deployment period produces a true "golden age" or merely a "gilded age" depends on institutional choices made at the turning point. The New Deal and Bretton Woods institutions created the framework for the postwar golden age of mass production. The lack of comparable institutional innovation after the 1870s crashes produced the Gilded Age — high growth but extreme inequality and instability. After the 2000 dotcom crash, the turning point was aborted by the Iraq War, the housing bubble, and the 2008 financial crisis. Perez argues that as of 2002, the information revolution's synergy phase (golden age) has not yet arrived because the institutional recoupling has not been completed.

**Finding 5 — The geography of revolutions shifts between core and periphery.** Each revolution originates in a specific country or region (Britain for the first two, US and Germany for the third, US for the fourth and fifth) and propagates outward. Follower nations can leapfrog during the irruption phase of a new revolution — the US and Germany overtook Britain during the third revolution (steel/electricity), establishing a "triangular" pattern of competition. The fifth revolution is the first truly global one, with the information paradigm propagating simultaneously to multiple regions. China's rise during the current revolution's installation period mirrors the US/Germany pattern of the 1870s.

## 3. BOUNDARY CONDITIONS

**When the model applies strongly:**
- Periods of genuine technological revolution — clusters of interrelated innovations that together constitute a new general-purpose technology with economy-wide applicability. The model requires a *system* of innovations, not a single breakthrough.
- Capitalist economies with developed financial markets capable of generating speculative dynamics. The installation-frenzy-crash sequence depends on the existence of liquid markets in financial assets.
- Long time horizons. The model describes 50–60 year cycles; it is not useful for analyzing year-to-year fluctuations or individual investment decisions.

**When it weakens or breaks:**
- When the "turning point" fails to produce institutional recoupling. Perez's model predicts a golden age after each crash, but she acknowledges this is contingent on political will and institutional innovation. The Gilded Age (1870s–1900s) is her own counter-example: decades of turbulence without a proper synergy phase. The current revolution may be repeating this pattern.
- In non-capitalist or hybrid economies where the financial-capital / production-capital distinction does not map cleanly. China's state-directed capitalism, where the government is simultaneously the source of financial capital, the regulator, and the operator of production capital, challenges the model's clean separation.
- For incremental innovations within a paradigm. Perez's model is about revolutionary transitions between paradigms, not about the continuous improvement that occurs within a paradigm. It does not explain why some firms within a given paradigm outperform others.

**Limitations Perez does not fully address:**
- The model is descriptive, not predictive. Perez can identify that a turning point has occurred only in retrospect. The four-phase framework tells you where you have been, not precisely where you are going.
- The model assumes each revolution is discrete and sequential. In reality, the information revolution may overlap with an emerging biotech/green revolution in ways that complicate the clean phasing.
- Perez's five revolutions are drawn entirely from the experience of Western capitalism (Britain, US, Germany). Whether the model applies to China's state-led innovation trajectory or to the possible sixth revolution she hints at (biotech/green) remains untested.

## 4. INTELLECTUAL GENEALOGY

**Builds on:** Schumpeter's theory of creative destruction and the entrepreneur as agent of discontinuous innovation. Kondratieff's long-wave theory (~50-year cycles of economic expansion and contraction), which Perez transforms from a mechanistic cycles-theory into a historically grounded analysis of techno-economic paradigm shifts. Kuhn's structure of scientific revolutions — Perez explicitly borrows the concept of "paradigm" and applies it to economic and technological organization. Marx's analysis of the relationship between productive forces and social relations — Perez shares Marx's insight that technological change reshapes social organization, but rejects historical determinism in favor of contingent outcomes. Freeman's concept of national systems of innovation. Dosi's concept of technological paradigms and trajectories.

**Responds to:** Mainstream neoclassical economics, which treats technological change as exogenous and assumes continuous equilibrium. Perez's entire framework is built on the premise that technological revolutions are endogenous, discontinuous, and disequilibrating. She also responds to crude Kondratieff cycle theory by insisting that the cycles are not mechanistically determined but depend on institutional choices at the turning point — the same technology can produce a golden age or a gilded age depending on political decisions.

**Contradicts:** Efficient market theory and rational expectations, which cannot account for the systematic decoupling of financial asset prices from productive value during the frenzy phase. Fogel-style cliometrics that evaluate infrastructure investment solely by marginal transportation cost savings (missing the paradigm-wide transformation). Linear models of innovation that treat the path from invention to commercialization as a pipeline rather than an evolutionary, waste-generating process.

**Connects forward to:** Janeway's Three-Player Game (Janeway explicitly adopts Perez's framework as his "explanatory schema" and reproduces her table and figures). Mazzucato's entrepreneurial state (Perez provides the macro-historical framework; Mazzucato provides the institutional analysis). Mallaby's account of VC as an institution adapted to fund the frenzy phase. Nicholas's evidence on geographic clustering maps onto Perez's core-periphery dynamics. Berlin's account of five simultaneous industry births in the 1970s describes the irruption phase of Perez's fifth revolution.

## 5. STEEL-MANNED VERSION

The most powerful version of Perez's thesis is this: **capitalism does not err when it produces bubbles — it functions.** The speculative frenzy is not a pathology to be cured but a *necessary mechanism* for funding the installation of revolutionary infrastructure whose value cannot be calculated in advance. Every golden age in capitalist history was built on the wreckage of a preceding financial mania that *overfunded* the infrastructure the golden age then exploited.

The canal mania of the 1790s built the canal system that powered the first industrial revolution's deployment. The railway manias of the 1840s and 1880s built the railway networks that enabled the mass-market economy. The electrification bubble of the 1920s built the power grids that made mass production possible. The dotcom bubble built the broadband infrastructure that enables the digital economy. In every case, the investors lost money — but society gained irreplaceable infrastructure.

This means the relevant policy question is not "how do we prevent bubbles?" (you cannot, and the attempt would starve revolutionary technology of funding) but "how do we ensure that the turning point produces a golden age rather than a gilded age?" Perez's answer: active institutional innovation that recouples financial capital with production capital, creates a regulatory framework appropriate to the new paradigm, and ensures that the gains from the deployment period are broadly shared. The New Deal and Bretton Woods are the model; their absence after the 2000 crash explains why the information revolution's golden age has not yet arrived.

The meta-insight is that **the same capitalism that creates the problem also creates the solution** — but only if the political system rises to the occasion at the turning point. Financial capital's destructive frenzy installs the infrastructure; production capital's patient deployment exploits it; regulation at the turning point manages the transition. Remove any element and the cycle stalls.

## 6. DANGEROUS BLIND SPOT

Perez's framework has a structural optimism problem: **it assumes the turning point will eventually arrive.** The model's explanatory power comes from its ability to identify a recurring pattern across five revolutions. But "recurring" is not "inevitable." Perez acknowledges that the turning point requires active institutional innovation — yet her model provides no mechanism for explaining why such innovation occurs in some cases (New Deal) and fails in others (Gilded Age, and arguably the post-2008 period).

The deeper issue is that the model treats financial capital and production capital as if they were symmetric forces that naturally alternate in dominance. But the financialization of the economy since the 1970s may represent a structural break, not a cyclical phenomenon. If financial capital has permanently captured the regulatory state — through lobbying, revolving doors, campaign finance, and intellectual hegemony (the very process Janeway documents with the Mont Pelerin Society) — then the recoupling that Perez's model requires at the turning point may never occur. The pattern breaks not because the theory is wrong but because one player has permanently rigged the game.

There is also a classification problem. Perez's model requires identifying discrete "technological revolutions" — clusters of innovations that constitute a new paradigm. But the boundary between revolutions is not always clear. Is AI a continuation of the fifth revolution (information and telecommunications) or the beginning of a sixth? Is green energy a revolution or a policy initiative? If the current period represents two overlapping revolutions, Perez's clean phasing breaks down entirely.

Finally, Perez's model is silent on the *content* of the turning point's institutional innovation. She specifies that it must recouple financial and production capital, broaden participation in prosperity, and create a regulatory framework for the new paradigm. But these are outcomes, not mechanisms. The model cannot explain why the US produced the New Deal after 1929 but has produced nothing comparable after 2008. Perez would argue the golden age is still possible — but she wrote that in 2002, and twenty-four years later the institutional innovation she predicted has not materialized.

## 7. CONNECTIONS TO CURRENT REALITY

**AI and the question of phasing.** If AI is a continuation of the fifth revolution, we are in a late-frenzy or early-deployment phase — the infrastructure (cloud computing, broadband, mobile devices, data centers) was installed during the dotcom era, and the current AI wave represents the beginning of the synergy phase. If AI is a *new* sixth revolution, we are in early irruption — and the speculative frenzy is still ahead. The distinction has enormous investment implications. Perez's framework suggests that $100B+ AI infrastructure investments (GPU clusters, data centers) look more like installation-period infrastructure buildout than deployment-period productivity gains — implying the current AI bubble is structurally necessary and that its crash, when it comes, will leave behind the infrastructure for the next golden age.

**PE-ification of venture as a frenzy-phase symptom.** Perez's model predicts that during the frenzy phase, financial capital becomes "self-sufficient" and "governs the casino." The explosion of multi-stage mega-funds, crossover investors, and the Unicorn Bubble are textbook frenzy-phase phenomena: financial innovation decoupled from productive purpose, asset prices divorced from cash flows, and an ever-expanding array of financial instruments (SPACs, rolling funds, continuation vehicles) designed to keep the casino operating. The PE-ification of venture is not a new institutional form — it is the latest expression of financial capital's perennial tendency to decouple during installation periods.

**Solo capitalists and the irruption-phase pattern.** Perez's model shows that new forms of risk capital always emerge during irruption to fund the new paradigm. The whaling agents, the railway promoters, the early auto financiers, and the original venture capitalists all emerged as new institutional forms adapted to new technological opportunities. Solo capitalists, rolling funds, and scout networks are the irruption-phase risk capital forms of the current moment — adapted to the specific characteristics of software's near-zero marginal cost and the internet's distribution capabilities.

**The missing turning point.** Perez's most consequential prediction — made in 2002 — was that the dotcom crash represented the turning point, and that institutional reform would soon produce the information revolution's golden age. Twenty-four years later, this has not happened. Instead: the 2008 financial crisis (a second crash, unprecedented in the model), political polarization, regulatory paralysis, and the concentration of digital platform power. The missing turning point is the single most important anomaly in Perez's framework — and the central challenge for anyone trying to apply it today.

**Geopolitics and the core-periphery dynamic.** Perez's model predicts that follower nations can leapfrog during the irruption phase of a new revolution. China's massive investment in AI, green energy, and digital infrastructure during what may be the irruption/frenzy of a sixth revolution (or the late installation of the fifth) is structurally analogous to the US and Germany overtaking Britain during the steel/electricity revolution of the 1870s. Perez's framework suggests this is not an anomaly but a recurring feature of technological revolutions — and that the US faces the same risk of being overtaken that Britain faced in the 1890s if it fails to execute its own turning point.
