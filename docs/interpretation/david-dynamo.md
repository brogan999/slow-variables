# Why a fast technology shows up slowly

A note on how the tracker reads Paul David's "The Dynamo and the Computer: An Historical Perspective on the Modern Productivity Paradox" (*American Economic Review* Papers and Proceedings, May 1990). It organises what the tracker measures; it supplies no figures.

**The puzzle.** David, an economic historian at Stanford, wrote at a time when computers were everywhere except in the productivity statistics. He looked for a precedent and found one in the electric dynamo, which took decades after the first power stations to register in American manufacturing productivity.

**The mechanism was the building, not the motor.** Early factory electrification mostly kept the old layout: "group drive", in which a motor replaced the steam engine but still turned line shafts and belts. The large gains came from "unit drive", a motor on each machine, which let factories drop the overhead shafting, build lighter single-storey plants and lay the floor out around the flow of materials. David is explicit that the wider opportunity "awaited the further physical depreciation of durable factory structures" and a general investment boom in the 1920s. Old plant stayed in use because it was still standing and already paid for.

**Why the tracker cares.** The diffusion lens is David's argument turned into measurements. Capability (the methods bucket) is the motor; adoption, reorganised work and productivity are the building. A fast capability clock alongside slow adoption and adaptation clocks is what this account predicts for a general-purpose technology, so the gap between them is evidence about the complements, not a verdict on the technology.

**What would break the analogy.** If AI needs no new building, for instance because it slots into existing software and roles without reorganisation, the lag should be short and the adaptation and productivity series should move within a few years of adoption. The thesis monitor's falsification test is where that shows up.
