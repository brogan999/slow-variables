# What makes an advantage last

A note on how the tracker reads durable advantage through Hamilton Helmer's seven powers. It organises what the tracker measures; it supplies no figures.

**Power is a benefit behind a barrier.** Hamilton Helmer, a strategy consultant and investor who studied why some firms keep earning more than their investors could expect from putting the money elsewhere at the same risk (their cost of capital), set out his account in 7 Powers (2016). A lasting advantage needs two things at once: a benefit, something that raises the firm's prices or lowers its costs, and a barrier, something that stops rivals copying it even when they can see it working. A benefit with no barrier is competed away; a barrier with no benefit protects nothing.

**The seven.** Helmer found seven combinations that recur:

- Scale economies: unit costs fall as volume grows, and a rival cannot match them without matching the volume.
- Network economies: each user makes the product more valuable to the others, so the leader's lead feeds itself.
- Counter-positioning: a newcomer adopts a model the incumbent could copy only by damaging its own business, so it does not.
- Switching costs: a customer would lose money, time or work by moving to a rival.
- Branding: buyers pay more for the same thing because of what they believe about who made it.
- Cornered resource: preferential access to something others need, on attractive terms: a patent, a licence, a scarce input, a rare team.
- Process power: an organisation's way of working that improves costs or quality and takes years for a rival to learn.

**The order they arrive in.** Helmer also found that the powers open at different stages. When a business starts, only counter-positioning and a cornered resource are available, since nothing else has had time to build. As it takes off, scale, network economies and switching costs become possible. Once it is stable, branding and process power are what can still be built. An advantage that is transitional at the start becomes durable only if the firm uses the window to build one of the later powers before the window shuts.

**How it maps onto the tracker's rent kinds.** A rent is profit above what it takes to keep a business going, earned because something holds rivals off. The site's rent rule sorts such profits into scarcity rents, scale and network rents, switching-cost rents and regulatory rents. Scarcity rents are a cornered resource that lasts only as long as the shortage. Scale and network rents are Helmer's first two powers. Switching-cost rents are his fourth. Regulatory rents are a cornered resource granted by a licence. So a company holding a scarce input today, which the bottleneck scorecard can see, has a transitional power; the durable question is whether it converts that into switching costs, scale or a licence before the shortage eases.

**What the tracker reads.** Concentration inside a layer and gross margins show where a benefit is holding against rivals. The open-weights lag and the falling price of fixed capability show where a barrier is failing. The tightness words show which cornered resources are still scarce.

**What the tracker cannot see.** Whether buyers use one vendor or several, the clearest reading of switching costs, is not yet measurable from public sources, and branding and process power leave no filed trace. The tracker can say where a power is being tested, not prove that one exists.
