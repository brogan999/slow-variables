# What sets the speed of adoption

A note on how the tracker reads Everett Rogers' *Diffusion of Innovations* (first edition 1962) and the Iowa hybrid-corn study it grew from. It organises what the tracker measures; it supplies no figures.

**Where it started.** Bryce Ryan and Neal Gross, rural sociologists at Iowa State, published "The Diffusion of Hybrid Seed Corn in Two Iowa Communities" (*Rural Sociology*, 1943): an obviously better seed still took years to spread from the first adopters to nearly every farm. Rogers, who grew up on a farm in Carroll, Iowa, had watched his own father hold out against hybrid corn until the drought of the 1930s made the difference impossible to ignore. He trained in rural sociology at Iowa State and spent his career generalising the pattern across farming, medicine, education and technology.

**Perceived attributes, not quality.** Rogers' synthesis is that adoption speed depends on how an innovation looks to the person deciding: its relative advantage over what they use now, its compatibility with their existing values and practices, its complexity, its trialability (whether it can be tried on a small scale first) and its observability (whether its results are visible to others). A technically superior innovation that is hard to try and hard to see working spreads slowly.

**Why the tracker cares.** The early-adoption bucket measures the first step Rogers describes, a firm or person saying yes, and keeps it apart from adaptation, the work actually changing. Generative AI scores unusually well on trialability (a chat window costs nothing to try) and poorly on observability inside firms (the gains land in individual workflows nobody else sees), which is one reading of why use can be broad while measured reorganisation stays thin.

**Limits.** Rogers describes individual and organisational decisions; he does not predict aggregate productivity, and the S-shaped adoption curve says nothing about how deep each adoption goes. The tracker therefore never reads a rising adoption share as evidence of economic impact on its own.
