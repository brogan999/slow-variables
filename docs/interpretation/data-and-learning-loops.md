# When data is a moat

A note on how the tracker reads data, learning loops and context as a source of advantage, through Kenneth Arrow and through Andrei Hagiu and Julian Wright. It organises what the tracker measures; it supplies no figures.

**Information is hard to own.** Kenneth Arrow, an American economist who studied how markets allocate resources and won the Nobel prize in economics in 1972, argued in "Economic Welfare and the Allocation of Resources for Invention" (1962) that information behaves unlike other goods. One person's use does not use it up, copying it is cheap, and a buyer cannot judge what it is worth without being told it, at which point they no longer need to buy it. So whoever produces knowledge struggles to keep the returns from it. A trained model is knowledge in this sense: its behaviour can be observed, imitated and, through a rival training on its outputs, partly copied.

**Most data is weaker protection than it looks.** Andrei Hagiu, who studies platforms at Boston University, and Julian Wright, an economist at the National University of Singapore who studies platform competition, asked in "When Data Creates Competitive Advantage" (Harvard Business Review, 2020) when learning from customers' data really keeps rivals out. Their answer was a set of tests. Data protects a firm when the value it adds stays high as the customer base grows, rather than flattening early; when it keeps its value rather than going stale quickly; when it is proprietary rather than available to anyone who asks; when the improvements it produces are hard to copy without the data itself; and when what the firm learns from one customer improves the product for the others. A learning loop that fails these tests gives a head start, not a moat.

**Context is different from data.** A great deal of what makes an AI system useful inside a firm is context: how that firm's work is done, its records, its exceptions, the judgements its people make. On Hagiu and Wright's tests that context often helps only the customer it came from, so it does not compound across customers. But it is specific in Oliver Williamson's sense, worth much less anywhere else, which makes it a source of switching costs for whoever holds it inside the customer's systems. The durable asset is often not the data but the position in the workflow that keeps collecting it.

**Where verification fits.** Data that checks whether an answer was right, graded outcomes from real work, is scarcer than data that shows what was said. It improves the product for every customer and goes stale slowly where the work does not change, so it passes more of the tests than most data does. That is why the site treats verified data as a candidate scarce input.

**What the tracker reads.** The open-weights lag and the price of fixed capability show how quickly knowledge in models leaks to rivals, which is Arrow's problem made visible. Token shares across models and routers show whether buyers stay with one supplier. The bottleneck scorecard reads training data as an input, when a gauge can feed it.

**What the tracker cannot see.** No firm publishes how much proprietary data it holds or how much its product improves per customer, so the tests above can be applied only by judgement. Answers that use them should say so.
